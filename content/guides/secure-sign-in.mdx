Some DNS providers offer no sign-in a third party can use.
Without help, their customers create an API key or type records into the provider's panel by hand.
**Secure sign-in** gives them a shorter way.
Your customer signs in to the provider in a temporary browser that a browser hosting company runs for a few minutes.
Then, where the provider allows it, our software makes a lasting [API key](#lasting-api-keys) at the provider, and CustomDomain™ keeps it.
The domain then connects through the provider's API and stays correct from then on.
Where no key can be made, our software makes the changes on the provider's DNS page for that one domain, in the same session.
Either way, the browser is closed at the end.
Your customer never sees that browser, because every screen they use is one of ours.
The [few pages that stay the provider's](#no-live-page) are listed below.

<Callout title="Where it is on">
  Secure sign-in runs only where it is switched on for the domain's provider.
  The hosted service has it on for every workspace at these providers: Namecheap, GoDaddy, IONOS, Name.com, Squarespace, Hover, Spaceship, Hostinger, OVHcloud and OpenSRS.
  At OVHcloud it covers the European manager, and OVHcloud US accounts are not served.
  At all but OpenSRS, the address of the DNS page names the domain.
  So the session can only open that one domain's records.
  At OpenSRS your customer signs in to the [Manage Web Interface](#opensrs), whose sign-in is for one domain.
  We fill in the domain, so the whole session is that domain.
  At Network Solutions, Dynadot, NameSilo, Gname, ResellerClub, Porkbun, Wix and Alibaba Cloud's international site, the DNS page's address is not confirmed yet.
  There it runs in supervised test sessions only, and is not offered to your workspace until it is confirmed.
  Onamae.com, Registro.br, REG.RU and Alibaba Cloud's China site are not offered.
  Their dashboards are not in English and have not been checked signed in.
  You can [connect REG.RU with an API key](/docs/dns/provider-setup#registrars-and-dns-hosts) instead.
  `POST /v1/domains:check` says so per domain in `rails.cloud_browser`, present only where the deployment has it on.
  When `available` is `false`, `reason` says why.
  `not_enabled_for_workspace` and `provider_not_listed` mean it is not offered here.
  `oauth_available` means the provider has a sign-in of its own, which wins.
  `site_not_supported` means the provider keeps each account on one country site, and we don't serve the visitor's site yet.
  The widget and the console offer it only when it is available.
</Callout>

## Who can start it

A person is always there. Only two callers may start a secure sign-in:

1. **The widget**, in your customer's browser, with a [widget token](/docs/authentication/widget-tokens) minted for the domain and the customer's `end_user_ref`.
   A token bound to no domain, or only to other domains, cannot start it.
   Its domain check answers `available: false` with `token_not_bound`.
   A start with it is refused with `403 token_not_bound`, and the widget shows the manual setup.
   The fix is a token for this domain: mint it with `domain` and `end_user_ref`.
   The widget's `getToken` is where your page hands it one.
   A token for a domain another customer connected is refused with `403 token_wrong_end_user`.
   Both answers carry a plain `title` for the person, and what to mint in `details`.
2. **The console**, from a domain's Connect page.
   It is offered there the way the widget offers it: only when neither a sign-in of the provider's own nor one-click setup runs.
   When the server recommends it, **Sign in to GoDaddy** (with the provider's name) is the one open card, with **Open secure sign-in**.
   An API key and the records by hand are closed rows under **Other ways to connect**.

An AI agent, the MCP server and a plain API call are refused with
`403 browser_customer_present_only`, and their domain check never offers it
(`caller_cannot_start`).

## What your customer sees

The examples below use Namecheap as the provider and `acme.com` as the domain.
In the widget, the sign-in screen is one card named **Secure sign-in**, with the button **Open secure sign-in**.
Under **Other ways to connect** sits the row **Add the records**.
If your customer closes the window or the sign-in fails, the card stays, with one line.
It reads "You didn't finish signing in. You can try again." or "That sign-in didn't work. You can try again."
The popup stays open and says why in its own window.
**Add the records** opens the records screen, which has a **Back** to the card.
On a computer, the sign-in opens in a popup the size of every other provider sign-in: 680 by 760, centered over the page.
On a phone or a tablet, it opens as a sheet over the page.
The sheet follows the visible part of the screen, so the keyboard never covers a field.

A bar across the top names the provider.
Sometimes one of the provider's own pages is on screen: a human check, a Google or Apple page, or the provider's terms or order.
Then the bar also shows the site that page is on.
It shows a tick when the site is the provider's own.
When it is not, it shows a warning: "This page is not on Namecheap's site. Do not type your password here."
The popup's words are in the widget's 13 languages.

### No live page [#no-live-page]

Since November 2, 2026, your customer does not see the provider's browser session, for any provider.
The sign-in, a code, an emailed link, a wait for an approval on a phone and the provider's own device checks are all screens of ours.
We read what the provider's page asks for, and ask your customer for it on our screen.
The provider's real page appears only where a security rule keeps it there:

| Where the provider's page still shows | Why |
| --- | --- |
| A human check, such as a CAPTCHA or "Verify you are human" | Only your customer may answer it, on the real page. |
| An identity provider's own sign-in (Google, Apple, Microsoft, Facebook, GitHub), with its account chooser and consent | It is your customer's own step. Our software never touches an identity provider's page. |
| The provider's terms or an order, such as an API agreement or IONOS's free order | Only your customer accepts a provider's terms or places an order. |
| Account details the provider needs before it makes a key, such as the postal address Gandi asks for on an organization | Only your customer gives their own details. Our software never types an address, a phone number or any other personal detail. |
| A password the provider asks for again after the sign-in, at a provider whose prompt we have not reviewed | Where we have reviewed the prompt, our own password screen asks for it and relays it from your customer's browser. |

**Pressed for your customer.** Some provider pages ask nothing and only want a press.
Our software presses them while your customer signs in, and the popup keeps saying "Signing you in".
At Name.com there are two such pages.
One is the device check ("Unrecognized device"), where it presses **Send me the code** when there is one way to be reached.
The other is **Remember this device?**, where it picks **Yes** and presses **Save and continue**.
It does this only on a short list of reviewed screens, recognized by their own text on the provider's own site.
It never chooses between two ways to be reached, and never presses a button that is not on the list.
It never touches a human check or an identity provider's page.
Your customer then lands on our **Enter your code** screen.

**A code asked inside the provider's frame.** Squarespace asks "Verify to continue" before a DNS change.
That box comes from Squarespace and sits over its own page.
Our software presses **Send code** when that is the only choice, and the popup shows our **Enter your code** screen.
Your customer types the code there.
Our software types it into Squarespace's box and presses **Verify**.
If Squarespace refuses the code, our screen says so and asks again.
The code is kept nowhere.
It goes to the run that is waiting for it and is forgotten, and it is in no log or record.

Sometimes a page cannot be shown as a screen of ours: a page our software cannot read, a stall, or an unknown page.
Then the popup ends on its own "We cannot continue the sign in here." screen.
It offers **Try again** and **Do the steps by hand**.
It never falls back to the provider's page.

### Our own sign-in form

The popup opens on a sign-in form of ours, laid out like the providers' own sign-in pages.
It shows the provider's logo, a large **Sign in**, and the first field named the way the provider names it.
Then come the password (hidden, with **Show**) and a full-width **Sign in**.
Each field is an outlined box with its name inside it.
Below them, under "or sign in with", the provider's own Google, Apple and other sign-ins form a row of square buttons with each one's logo.
They appear where the provider has them.
**Forgot password?** is at the foot.
There is no line of text under the logo, and this first screen has no link to the provider's page.

The first field's label is the provider's own, in all 13 languages.
GoDaddy uses "Username or Customer #", with GoDaddy's own **Use email** switch.
IONOS uses "Customer ID, email address or domain".
Namecheap, Name.com, Hover and Spaceship use "Username".
Squarespace and Hostinger use "Email address", Network Solutions uses "User ID", and Wix uses "Email".
IONOS and Wix ask for the username first and the password on a second page, and we ask the same way.
Your customer types the username alone and presses **Next**, then types the password.
If the provider answers that there is no such account, its answer shows right there, over **Next**.
Sometimes the provider asks for an emailed code in between.
IONOS does on a new device, and the remote browser always is one.
Then the code screen comes first, and the form goes on to the password after.

Your customer types into our fields.
Their own browser passes each key to the provider's real sign-in page in the remote browser.
At **Sign in**, the provider's own button is pressed.
The form shows at once.
If the provider's page is still opening when your customer presses **Sign in**, a line under the button says so ("Opening Namecheap's sign in…").
The sign-in goes ahead as soon as the page is ready.

If the provider turns the sign-in down, its own message shows on our form, such as "That password is incorrect".
The message is at most 120 characters, with email addresses and long numbers masked.
The password is emptied and the username is kept.

**Forgot password?** opens the provider's own password reset page in a new tab of your customer's own browser.
It never opens in the remote browser, so the reset email's link opens there too.
Back in the popup, the password field asks for the new one ("Use your new password.").

The form is built as a real sign-in form.
Each field carries the provider's name ("GoDaddy Username or Customer #").
So a password manager's search finds the customer's saved login for the provider.
A manager will not offer it by itself, because the form is on our site, not the provider's.

### When our form cannot go on

Our form is the way in.
When it cannot do the job, the popup ends on its "We cannot continue the sign in here." screen, with **Try again** and **Do the steps by hand**:

- we have no reviewed form for that provider;
- nothing we recognize happens for 10 seconds after **Sign in** or **Next**;
- the provider shows something our screens cannot represent (a field that is not
  a username, a password or a code), twice in a row;
- the connection that carries the keys to the remote browser does not open.

Older versions moved to the provider's own page here, with "Continue on
Namecheap's page" across the foot, and offered **Try on Namecheap's page**. They
no longer do.

Sometimes the popup shows the provider's page: a human check, a Google or Apple sign-in, or the provider's terms or order.
It brings the part that matters into view in a rounded frame of ours, such as the sign-in card or the check.
A margin of the real page stays around it, so nothing beside it is cut off at an edge.
A page laid out for a phone keeps its whole width.
A page with nothing to bring forward shows edge to edge.

### Codes, links and approvals

The remote browser is a new device to every provider, so most providers ask for
a code. Each prompt gets one screen of ours:

| The provider asks for | The popup shows |
| --- | --- |
| A code by email, text or authenticator app | Where the code went ("Check your email for the code from Namecheap."), a code field, **Paste** and **Continue**. A code the provider asks for in a frame of its own after the sign-in (Squarespace's "Verify to continue") gets the same screen, **Enter your code**. |
| A sign-in link by email | "Open the email from Namecheap and tap the link, then come back here." When the customer comes back, the provider's page is nudged once. If it still waits 8 seconds later: "Signed in on this device instead? Paste the link from the email." |
| An approval on another device | "Approve the sign in on your phone", with nothing to press. |
| A passkey or a security key | "Passkeys cannot be used here" and **Use another way**. A passkey cannot reach a browser on another machine. |

The code field is a one-time code field, so a phone's offer to fill a code from Messages or Mail reaches it.
Until it holds a code, a **Paste** button stands where **Continue** will be.
A tap on it reads the clipboard and takes the first code in it.
A code is 4 to 10 digits, also written "482 913" or "482-913", and never part of a longer number such as a phone number.
It puts the code in the field.
When the provider's page said how many digits the code has, and the pasted code has that many, it is sent at once.
Nothing read from the clipboard is kept, logged or reported.
Where the provider also emails a sign-in link, **Paste the link instead** is there too.

After **Continue** the screen says "Checking the code" until the provider answers,
and then it says what the answer was:

- **The provider refused the code.** The field comes back at once, outlined in red, with the provider's own message under it ("The security code is invalid").
  If the provider takes long, the screen says "Still checking with IONOS" from 8 seconds.
  If it still asks for the code 20 seconds later and says nothing, we show our own line: "IONOS did not accept that code. Enter the newest one."
  Each new session makes the provider send a new code.
  So a code from an earlier email is the usual reason.
- **The code found no field on the provider's page.** Nothing is typed.
  A code typed where the page has no code field could land in its search box.
  The screen says it did not go through, and offers **Try again** and **Do the steps by hand**.
- **The provider took the code.** The next screen says **Code accepted**.
  At IONOS, which asks for its code before the password, it shows over the password.
  Elsewhere it shows under "Signing you in".

While the provider's page loads after a code, the screen stays at "Checking the code".
It never says "Signing you in" for a code the provider may still refuse.

Codes take time to arrive, so a code or a link keeps the session open.
The sign-in window runs at least 10 minutes from the moment the prompt appears.
The whole session can run 20 minutes instead of 15.

### Google, Apple and other sign-ins

Some provider sign-in pages offer "Sign in with Google", or Apple, Facebook, Amazon and the like.
Where we have reviewed that provider's sign-ins, our form shows the same ones.
GoDaddy's Google, Apple, Facebook and Amazon buttons are an example.
Each shows as a square button with its logo, and a screen reader reads "Continue with Google".
Pressing one presses the provider's own, and the identity provider's page shows in the popup.
That includes a window it opens, as Squarespace's does, sized to your customer's screen like the provider's page.
Your customer signs in there.
**Back to GoDaddy** brings back our form.
It steps the tab back when the sign-in took the whole tab, or closes the identity provider's window when it opened one.
Leaving Google's page without signing in brings our form back by itself after a few seconds.
Google may refuse to sign the browser in ("Couldn't sign you in").
Then the popup shows its own screen instead: "Google won't let this browser sign in".
It offers **Use my GoDaddy password**, a link to set a password at the provider when it has a reset page, and another way to set it up.
Only your customer goes there.
The software that makes the changes never visits an identity provider's page.

Sometimes no Google window appears.
Some pages use the browser's own account chooser, and a fresh browser has no account for it.
Then the customer signs in with the provider's password instead.
Google, Apple and Facebook can also refuse a sign-in from a browser they have not seen before.
The provider's password works then too.

### Human checks

Some providers show a "Verify you are human" check to a browser they have not seen before.
This is one of the pages that stays the provider's.
The popup shows the provider's page, zoomed to the check, with **Tick the box**, and your customer ticks it.
For Kernel-hosted sessions, our own check screen comes first.
Our software never clicks, works around or hides a check.
The browser hosting company may answer a standard check by itself.
One it does not answer is your customer's to tick.
If it has not cleared after 45 seconds, **Try again** reloads the view.
**Set it up another way** leads to the manual steps.

### After the sign-in

While the provider finishes the sign-in, the popup says **Signing you in**.
That covers the provider's own redirects after the last step or the code.
It never shows the sign-in form again unless the provider asks for something.
Once the sign-in is through, the popup says **Signed in**.
Our software then makes the changes in one of two ways.

**The DNS page, then the key.** For most providers the records come first.
Our software goes straight to the provider's DNS page, makes the changes and checks them.
The popup says **Done.**, and your customer can close the window.
Only then does our software try to make a [lasting API key](#lasting-api-keys) on the provider's own API page.
It uses the same browser, asks your customer nothing, and has 150 seconds.
If it works, CustomDomain™ checks and keeps the key.
The domain moves to the provider's API from then on, with no second write.
If the key page asks for anything, the attempt ends, because nobody is watching any more.
That covers a code, a password, an agreement, an order or account details.
The records are already in place either way.
Until 2026-11-03 the key was made first.
It failed 17 of 21 times in production.
Each failure cost your customer 25 to 60 seconds of waiting before any record was touched.
**Done.** shows before the key's outcome is known.
So a key that was made and could not be kept is not named on that last screen.
It is named in your console's **Activity**, and in the session while it lives.

**The key first.** Some providers' key flow needs your customer.
It may need a sign-in on the key page, a code, an agreement to tick, an order, or account details.
These keep the older order.
The popup reads "Getting things ready" over the records still to add, with "This takes about half a minute."
Then it reads "Adding records", then **Done.**
Our software makes the key on the provider's own API page, and CustomDomain™ checks it and keeps it.
The records are then added through the provider's API, as for a key someone pasted.
The popup's own steps and the sign-in form do not mention an API or a key.
Your customer is told the work is being prepared, not how.
A provider's own page, such as IONOS's checkout, still says what it says.
Sometimes the key cannot be made.
There may be no reviewed API page, or the account may not qualify.
The account may already be at the provider's [key limit](#key-limits).
The key may fail its check, the API page may want a new sign-in, or the key may be turned off for that provider.
Then the same session goes on to the provider's DNS page for the domain, and nothing is kept.
So a later change at that provider needs another sign-in.

**Accounts that failed the key are not asked again.**
CustomDomain™ remembers, per workspace, person and provider, when an account answered "not eligible" for a lasting key.
It does not try the key again for that account for 14 days.
So the session starts at the DNS page, and the sign-in screen carries no key notice.
Two failures in a row of any other kind stop it for one day.
A key that is kept clears the memory.
The service holds this memory in its own memory, with only words and times.
So a restart forgets it and costs one more attempt.

**GoDaddy's key is paused for now.** Every GoDaddy attempt in the last two weeks ended without a key.
So a GoDaddy connect session starts on the DNS page, and no key is made or promised.
The text below about GoDaddy's developer portal describes the path as it works when switched back on.
Adding an account from the console is not affected.

**What your customer sees while it works.** Every waiting screen draws the same four steps: Sign in, Read records, Make changes and Check.
The step in progress turns, and the finished ones are ticked.
Under the list is one line saying what is happening, then an estimate, then the records.
The estimate appears only for the long steps.
Reading the records says "Usually under half a minute."
Making the changes says "Usually under a minute. Keep this window open."
Making the key says "This takes about half a minute."
A step can run past its usual time: 45 seconds reading, 60 for the key, 90 making the changes, or 45 checking.
Then the line changes to "Taking longer than usual. It is still working, so keep this window open."
Nothing that is still working is red.
A session that ended shows a neutral mark, one plain line that says what to do, and **Close**.
A whole successful run takes about one to two minutes.
That's why the screen says so instead of showing a spinner alone.

The DNS page path shows one line for each step over the records, which tick as they are made.
The lines are "Reading your DNS records", "Making the changes" and "Checking the result".

Your customer never loses a step.
Whatever stops the key, the records still get made in the same session, without another sign-in.
A key is tried once per session.
Once it has ended without a key, the session goes straight to the DNS page whenever the work goes on again.
That happens after a page was handed back, or after **Continue signing in**.
So no second key is made at the provider.
A new session tries the key again.
Some provider API pages send the browser through their sign-in page, as GoDaddy's developer site does.
Then our software waits up to 8 seconds for it to come back.
The provider may stay on a check that asks your customer to prove it's them, such as a code by text message.
The window then hands that page to your customer, as it does for a code on the DNS page.
Once they enter it, the work goes on, and the key is made.
If they don't answer in 10 minutes, the key is skipped.
After such a trip, our software waits up to 6 seconds for the page to change before it acts again.
So it sees a form the provider opens after its own check.

**GoDaddy (while its key is switched on).** When a lasting GoDaddy key can be made, the sign-in opens on GoDaddy's developer portal.
That is the Personal Access Token page, which GoDaddy's own instructions say to sign in to before a token is made.
Your customer signs in there as usual.
When **Generate Token** is pressed, GoDaddy asks to verify it's them with a code it texts ("Verify it's you").
The window shows that page, and your customer types the code.
Then our software fills in the token's form and makes the token.
The domain then connects through GoDaddy's API.
GoDaddy's cookie banner is accepted on the way, so it does not cover the page.
If no token can be made, the same window goes on to the domain's DNS page.
GoDaddy may ask to sign in again there.

**Read-only DNS records.** The provider's DNS page may show the records with every edit and delete control turned off.
An account that can view the domain but not change it is one example.
Our software then stops within a few seconds and changes nothing.
The session ends with that reason.

Nobody watches the provider's page after the sign-in.
If the provider asks for the password again, asks for a code, or shows a check in the middle of the work, it is handed back.
The popup says, for example, "Namecheap asks for your password again."
A code is asked on our **Enter your code** screen, and a check is ticked on the provider's page.
A password asked again is still the provider's page, until our own password screen relays it.
The work goes on once the prompt is gone.
An API page that asks the account holder to accept the provider's API terms is handed back too ([below](#the-providers-terms-are-your-customers)).

Software of ours does the work, picking one click at a time from an outline of the provider's page.
Some steps have worked the same way at that provider several times.
For those, it first follows that path again, with the values of the plan your customer approved.
Otherwise a fast model picks each click.
A stronger model takes over for the rest of the session when the fast one gets stuck.
Neither model chooses what is typed, because our code fills in each value.
They never see a password field or anything typed.
Email addresses, keys and secrets in the outline are masked.
Every click passes the same checks, whichever way it was chosen.
It may visit only the pages it needs, and make only the changes approved.

### When your customer approves a change [#review]

A plan that only **adds** records needs no approval screen.
The popup approves it by itself, once.
Your customer sees "Making the changes" and then **Done.**
The plan is still checked.
The popup sends the approval with a fingerprint (a hash) of the exact plan.
The control plane refuses it if the plan is not the one it made, as it does for an approval your customer presses.

In three cases your customer sees a **Review** screen first.
It reads "Review the changes for acme.com", with the exact changes.
Nothing changes until they press **Approve and make these changes**:

- the plan **replaces or removes** a record the domain already has;
- the plan changed after it was first made, so what your customer would approve
  is not what they were shown;
- an approval the popup sent by itself failed. It never sends a second one.

### Signed in to the wrong account

The changes can be made only in the account that holds the domain.
Your customer may sign in to an account that does not hold it, such as a personal account instead of the business one.
Then the popup says so on a screen of ours: "acme.com isn't in j\*\*\*@example.com's Namecheap account".
It reads "this Namecheap account" when the provider's page shows no name.
It offers **Sign in with another account**.
That signs the remote browser out of the provider, and opens our form again, empty.
From the second wrong account on, **Set it up another way** leads to the manual steps as well.
Three accounts can be tried.
The third that does not hold the domain ends the session (`browser_wrong_account`).

Before it asks, the DNS page is read twice, after a fresh load.
So a page that failed for a moment is not taken for a wrong account.
Some providers have an account switcher, such as GoDaddy's Delegate Access.
There our software first opens the account that holds the domain, when the switcher shows which one it is.
It types nothing there, and refuses any control that changes who has access.

The account name on that screen is the one the provider's page shows.
It may be a user name, a customer number or an address.
An address is masked to its first character and its domain.
Only your customer sees it, and it is never stored or logged.

### OpenSRS: a sign-in for one domain [#opensrs]

At OpenSRS, your customer signs in to OpenSRS' Manage Web Interface (`manage.opensrs.net`).
That is the panel for the person who owns the domain.
They use the user name and password that come with the domain, and both are case sensitive.
That sign-in is for one domain, so our software fills in the domain itself, before the page is shown.
The field cannot be changed.
Your customer types only the user name and the password, on our form as usual.
Before it reads the records, and before every change, our software checks that the page shows the domain.
If it shows another domain, the session ends with nothing changed.
The code is `browser_wrong_account`: "You signed in for a different domain at the provider, so nothing was changed."

Only OpenSRS' own address is covered.
A reseller that runs the same panel under its own brand and address is not covered.
Neither is a reseller's own control panel, or the Reseller Control Panel (`manage.opensrs.com`) that resellers use.
Those customers get the other ways to connect.
The records can be changed there only while the domain uses OpenSRS' own name servers.

The provider's page may show that the domain uses DNS somewhere else.
Then the popup says "acme.com does not use Namecheap's DNS, so its records are edited elsewhere."
The session ends with `browser_not_provider_dns`.
A provider may send such a domain away from its DNS page instead of saying so.
That ends the session too (see [When the setup cannot finish](#when-the-setup-cannot-finish)).

### When the setup cannot finish

Since October 21, 2026, the hosted service never hands the window over for your customer to add records by hand.
Our software may fail to finish on the provider's DNS page.
The page may be unreadable, it may not take a change, or it may show the records read only.
Then the session ends and the browser is closed.
The popup's last line is "The setup did not finish at GoDaddy. Close this window and try again."
The **Sign in to GoDaddy** button on the card starts a new session.
The connection stays pending, so if the records appear anyway, it completes as usual.
The session's `error.code` is `browser_script_failed` or `browser_verify_failed`.
It is `browser_zone_changed` when the records kept changing during review.

## The lasting API key [#lasting-api-keys]

An *API key* is a password for software.
The provider issues it, and whoever holds it can call the provider's API for the account.
After the sign-in, where the provider allows it, our software makes one on the provider's own API page.
CustomDomain™ keeps it.
The domain is then connected as if your customer had pasted a key in the widget.
CustomDomain™ writes its records, checks them, and puts back any that go missing.
All of this goes through the provider's API, with no browser and no customer, until the key nears its end.

| GoDaddy | A Personal Access Token with GoDaddy's whole Domains & DNS group | Transfer the domain away (`transfer`) | After 1 year, the longest GoDaddy offers |
| Namecheap | API access turned on for CustomDomain™'s address, and the account's own key | Everything Namecheap's API allows the account (`account`) | No end date |
| Name.com | A production API token (Name.com's tokens have no scopes) | Everything Name.com's API allows the account (`account`) | No end date |
| Spaceship | An API key and secret with every domain and DNS permission, transfers and billing included | Transfer the domain away, and make charges on the account: renewals and purchases (`transfer`, `billing`) | No end date |
| Hostinger | An API token (Hostinger's tokens have no scopes) | Everything Hostinger's API allows the account (`account`) | The longest Hostinger offers: none where it offers that, else 1 year or less |
| IONOS, US site only | An API key: a public prefix and a secret | Nothing: it reaches the DNS records only | No end date |

We have reviewed the key page at a few providers.
GoDaddy's is paused for now ([why](#after-the-sign-in)), and the others are on.
GoDaddy's was on with GoDaddy's permission.
Its published API terms require that permission for keys someone else makes for its customers.
When a key can't be made or checked, the session goes to the DNS page instead.
An account the provider won't give a key to is one example.
Every other provider, and IONOS's other country sites, has no key page yet, so its sessions use the DNS page.
At IONOS, the key is made in IONOS's developer portal.
We reach it through the control panel's own **Open API Portal** link, so your customer's sign-in carries over.
If the portal still asks them to sign in, the window shows that page to them.

Some accounts cannot have a key.
Examples are an account under Namecheap's API thresholds, or one GoDaddy says is not eligible.
The session then goes on to the DNS page.
Our software never changes a plan or turns on two step settings for your customer.
At Name.com, an account with two step verification needs a switch on its Security page before Name.com's API takes any token.
That switch is the account holder's to turn on.
So a session whose sign-in asked for a code makes no token and goes on to the DNS page.
That way, no token is left that could not work.

At Namecheap, the key is the account's own.
If the account's API access is off, our software turns it on and adds our address to its allowed list.
Namecheap then shows its API terms and asks for the account password, and both are your customer's.
The window shows Namecheap's page for them.
The sign-in form itself says nothing about an API.

A key can be made and then not kept.
The provider may refuse it when we check it, or the first change made with it may fail.
The key then stays in your customer's account, and most providers have no API to delete one.
So the window's last screen names it, with a link to the provider's page to delete it.
For example: "An API key named “CustomDomain App 2026-10-22 1528 3f9a” was made at GoDaddy and is not in use."
Your console's **Activity** page records it too, with the same name and link.
It reads "An API key we made is still in your GoDaddy account".
The key itself is never shown or kept.

You can tell your customer which key it is, and where to delete it, both before the sign-in and after.
Before it, the [domain check](/docs/api-reference/domains/checkDomain) answers `rails.cloud_browser.key`.
It has `name`, the name a key made now would get, such as "CustomDomain Acme 2026-10-30 1204".
That's your application's name, then the day and minute.
It also has `delete_url` (the provider's page that lists and deletes API keys), `rights` and `ends_in_days`.
A sign-in makes a key, or may reuse one of ours the account already has.
Each session names its key from the minute it starts, and adds a four character code of its own at the end ("CustomDomain Acme 2026-10-30 1206 3f9a").
So look for a key that begins with that name, not for that exact minute.
Namecheap's sign-in makes no key, because it turns on the account's own, so its `key` has no `name`.
After the sign-in, the session's view ([read a session](/docs/api-reference/cloud-browser/getBrowserSession)) carries `kept_key` once the key is kept.
It holds the key's `name`, the provider's `page` to delete it, its `rights` and `expires_at`.
`expires_at` is left out for a key with no end date.
It holds `reused: true` when no key was made, because the sign-in used the key we stored for the account.
That happens when the provider's page still listed its row ([below](#reusing-a-key-of-ours)).
A key that was made and not kept is `left_key` instead, never both.

IONOS's API is off for an account until the account signs up for it.
It is a free product in IONOS's shop, under IONOS's API terms.
When IONOS's key page says it is off, our software opens that free order for your customer.
So they never go into IONOS's panel for it.
The last step is theirs.
IONOS's checkout asks them to accept IONOS's general terms and to press Buy now.
That can place a temporary hold on their card, and we never accept either for them.
So the window shows them IONOS's checkout, with "Finish IONOS's free API order on the page: accept its terms and place it. We go on after."
They finish it there, and the key is made.
They have about 4 minutes.
They may step off the checkout and back, to add a payment method or when IONOS asks them to verify it's them.
Our software presses nothing on the checkout.
The line that used to sit under the sign-in button about IONOS's free order is gone.
The order is explained only where IONOS's checkout appears.
Our software never presses anything on a page that shows a price above zero, asks for payment details or asks to hold a sum on a card.
It ticks no box and types nothing.
If anything on IONOS's page is not as expected, the session goes on to the DNS page instead.

### A provider's key limit [#key-limits]

Some providers cap how many API keys one account can hold.
IONOS may allow about 5 per account.
We have not confirmed the number, so we work with 5 until we have.
CustomDomain™ counts the keys it made for each owner: your workspace, or one end user of one application.
A key the provider refuses, or that has ended, stops counting.
It never makes the last one under the limit, so your customer's own key still fits.
At IONOS it makes a key while 3 or fewer of ours are counted, and none once there are 4.

IONOS's keys page does not say which account a key belongs to.
So every IONOS account of one owner counts as one.
Say your team connected domains in four different IONOS accounts.
The next IONOS sign-in makes no key, even in a fifth account that holds none of ours.
That lasts until one of the four is deleted or ends.

At the limit, we use a key of ours again where the provider's page lists it ([below](#reusing-a-key-of-ours)).
IONOS's page does not list it, so there the session goes on to the DNS page instead.
No second key is made, and nothing is left in the account.
A reconnect that cannot make its new key for this reason ends, and the current key is unchanged.
Adding an account ends with "No account was added."

A reconnect or an added account that stops here says why.
For example: "IONOS allows about 5 API keys in one account and CustomDomain has already made 4 of them, so it did not make another. Delete the ones you no longer use on IONOS's keys page, then try again."
A connect does not stop, so your customer sees the DNS page and no message about the key.
Your console's **Activity** page records every stop as "Secure sign-in to IONOS did not make an API key".
It shows the same numbers and a link to the provider's keys page.
The keys we make are named "CustomDomain" and your application's name, so they are easy to pick out.
A key deleted on the provider's page stops counting once our daily check finds it refused.

### Every domain scope, said in `rights`

Since October 20, 2026, each key is made with every scope the provider's page offers for domains.
That means DNS and nameservers, and where the page offers them, transfers and (at Spaceship) billing.
Scopes for the provider's other products are never ticked.
Examples are GoDaddy's Commerce, Email and Hosting, and Spaceship's contacts, Seller Hub and Hyperlift.

So that you always know what a key could do, each key's `rights` lists what it
reaches beyond the domain's DNS records:

| `rights` | Means |
| --- | --- |
| `transfer` | It can transfer the domain away from the provider. |
| `billing` | It can make charges on the account: Spaceship's renewals and purchases. |
| `account` | The provider's keys have no scopes, so it holds every right the provider's API gives the account, transfers and renewals included where the API offers them. |
| (empty) | It reaches the DNS records only. |

The console says the same in a sentence on the domain's key card.
For example: "This key can also transfer the domain away from Spaceship, and make charges on the account (renewals and purchases)."

### The provider's terms are your customer's

Some API pages ask the account holder to accept the provider's API terms before a key is made.
Name.com's API Access Agreement and Reseller Agreement are one example.
Our software never accepts terms for anyone.
At Name.com, it types the token's name, then the popup shows Name.com's page.
The provider's terms are one of the pages that stay the provider's.
The popup adds one line: "Accept Name.com's API terms on the page and create the key there. The setup goes on once you have."
Your customer ticks the box and presses Create token.
The work goes on with the token in the row named as we named it.
It never uses Name.com's test token or another token of the account, and it waits up to 10 minutes.
If your customer does not accept them, the session goes on to the DNS page.

### Checked, then kept for one application

- **What is typed.** Our software types only two things.
  The first is the key's name: `CustomDomain`, your application's name, the day and minute it was made in UTC, and a four character code of the session's own.
  An example is "CustomDomain Acme 2026-10-21 1528 3f9a".
  So your customer can find it at the provider and tell a new key from an old one.
  They never meet two keys of one name, even from two sign-ins in one minute.
  The second, at Namecheap only, is CustomDomain™'s address.
  It ticks only the scopes above, picks the longest expiry the page offers, and presses only buttons we have reviewed.
- **Read once, never shown.** The key is read once from the page. The software
  that picks the clicks sees it masked (`[key1]`), and it is never logged, sent
  in an event or stored with the session.
- **Checked before it is kept.** CustomDomain™ makes one read only call with it
  (the domain's records). A key the provider does not answer is never kept: your
  application gets `credential.failed` and the session goes on to the DNS page.
- **Kept for one application.** A checked key is kept encrypted, like a key a person pastes in the console.
  It is kept as a [provider account](/docs/connect-flow/provider-accounts#keys-made-at-a-secure-sign-in) that belongs to the application whose domain it was made for.
  No other application can use it until an admin [shares it](/docs/connect-flow/provider-accounts#which-applications-may-use-it).
  Connecting another application's domain with it is refused with `403`.
  If writing the records then fails, our copy is deleted again and the session goes on to the DNS page.
- **Used again for your next domain.** You may connect another domain of the same application from the console.
  The connect page then offers the saved key above the sign-in, such as "Use the saved IONOS key".
  CustomDomain™ checks with the provider that the key reaches the new domain.
  It adds the domain to the key's domains and writes the records, with no sign-in and no new key.
  A domain in another account at the provider still needs its own sign-in.
  A key made when your customer signed in through the widget serves only their domain.
- **One key per provider account.** A second sign-in may be for a provider account the workspace already keeps a key for.
  It swaps its new key into that [provider account](/docs/connect-flow/provider-accounts#whose-key-it-is-and-saved-access) instead of adding another.
  GoDaddy is the exception for now: each sign-in there keeps its own key ([what that means](/docs/dns/provider-setup#reuse-of-godaddy-keys-is-held-for-now)).
  Where the provider's page still lists our earlier key, the sign-in uses that key instead of making one ([reusing a key of ours](#reusing-a-key-of-ours)).
- **It can also move a domain's nameservers.** A key kept this way counts as a connected account for [DNS hosting](/docs/dns/managed-dns).
  It does so for the application's domains only ([provider accounts](/docs/connect-flow/provider-accounts#dns-hosting-uses-an-account-at-the-registrar)).

### Reusing a key of ours [#reusing-a-key-of-ours]

Some provider key pages list each key with its name and the account it belongs to, such as Name.com's token table.
There, a second sign-in for the same provider account does not make another key.
We only look at the page, to see that the row of the key we made and stored the first time is still listed.
The row must match its exact name and its exact account, in one row.
If it does, we use the key we stored for it, check with the provider that it still works, and keep it in the same provider account as before.
We never read a key's value off the provider's page.
So a row another person or another application named like ours adds nothing to your account.
If the row is not there, or the provider refuses the stored key, a new key is made, as before.
The stored key may not be keepable after it was checked, because its row changed or went away in the meantime.
Then the sign-in goes on to the DNS page.
Your application does not get `credential.failed` with `not_kept`, because no key was made.
That reason is for a key that was.

The customer sees one difference: no new key appears in their account. The key
used has the rights and the end date it was made with, for the same provider
account, workspace and application. The session says so in `kept_key.reused`, and
the key's name is the earlier key's. GoDaddy never reuses a key, and neither do
providers whose page shows a key once, when it is made (Namecheap makes none).

### What you see

- `GET /v1/connections/{id}` carries `credential`.
  It holds the provider account that holds the key (`account_id`), `provider`, and `status` (`active`, `expiring`, `needs_reconnect` or `expired`).
  It also holds the key's `name`, `scopes` and `rights`.
  It holds when it was checked (`verified_at`) and when it ends (`expires_at`), plus the reconnect fields below.
  It never holds the key.
- The provider account carries `application_id`, `origin: "browser_agent"` and
  `agent_key`, the same record.
- Four webhooks: `credential.created`, `credential.expiring`,
  `credential.failed` and `credential.removed`
  ([event catalog](/docs/webhooks/overview#event-catalog)).
- In the console, the domain's page has a **Provider API key** card, and
  **Connected accounts** lists the key as **Made at sign in**
  ([console guide](/docs/getting-started/console#provider-api-key)).

### Reusing it for a returning customer [#saved-access]

A key a customer's own secure sign-in made can be offered to them for their next domain in the same application.
That saves a new sign-in.
Each application has a setting for it, `end_user_saved_access`, on by default.
With it on, a returning customer is offered a key they kept before, for their other domains.
Turn it off and no key is offered, so every domain starts with a sign-in.
The rules for which key may be offered, to whom, and what an offer can do for now are in [A returning customer's own key](/docs/connect-flow/provider-accounts#a-returning-customers-own-key).

- **In the console:** **Widget → Ways to connect → Use their saved access**, a
  switch that saves when you flip it. Owners, admins and members can change it;
  a viewer sees it off with the reason. A test mode application keeps no saved
  access (its sign-ins are made up), so its switch is off and cannot be turned
  on.
- **In the API:** `PATCH /v1/applications/{id}` with `end_user_saved_access`
  (`true` or `false`, never `null`). Every application answer carries it.
- **Whether secure sign-in runs for you:** every application answer also carries `secure_sign_in`, read only.
  It holds `available`, and when that is `false`, a `reason`.
  `switched_off` means the deployment has it off.
  `not_enabled_for_workspace` means it is on for a list of workspaces, and yours is not on it.
  **Ways to connect** shows it as **On** or **Off** with that reason.
  It says whether the feature can run for the workspace at all.
  Whether it is offered for one domain is still `rails.cloud_browser` on the domain check.

## When the key ends, and reconnecting [#reconnect]

A key with an end date (GoDaddy's after a year, Hostinger's where it offers no
"never") is watched every hour. The examples use Hostinger:

1. **30 and 7 days before its end**, three things happen.
   Your application gets `credential.expiring`, with `days_left` and `reconnect: true`.
   Your workspace's owners and admins get the notification **A DNS provider key ends soon**.
   Your customer gets an email, "Reconnect Hostinger for acme.com", when you gave us their address ([end user notices](/docs/connect-flow/end-user-notices#reconnect-emails)).
   The key keeps working until its end.
2. **At its end**, our copy is deleted. The domain stays connected and monitored,
   but missing records are not put back until your customer reconnects. Your
   application gets `credential.failed` (reason `expired`), and the owners and
   your customer are told again.
3. **When the provider stops accepting the key** (it answers `401` or `403`, for
   example because your customer deleted it), the same happens with reason
   `refused`.

A reconnect is one sign-in by your customer, and there are three ways to ask for
it:

- **A reconnect link.** `POST /v1/connections/{id}:reconnect-link` answers a `link`.
  The link opens CustomDomain™'s hosted page on the sign-in for the key's provider.
  It works once, and for 14 days.
  With `{"send": true}` and your customer's address on the connection, CustomDomain™ emails it on your behalf instead and answers `emailed: true`.
  The console's **Send reconnect link** and **Copy reconnect link** do the same.
  A connection with no such key answers `409 not_reconnectable`.
  Only your API key makes a link, because the link signs in as your customer.
  So a widget token, whatever end user it is bound to, is refused with `403`.
  An AI agent's token is refused too, whatever its scopes, and no link is made.
- **The widget**, in your own product: `open({ mode: "reconnect", connectionId })`
  with a [widget token](/docs/authentication/widget-tokens) minted for the domain
  and your customer's `end_user_ref`, as for any connect
  ([reference](/docs/widget-sdk/reference#open--openconfig)).
- **The email** CustomDomain™ sends by itself before the end, at it, or when the
  provider refuses the key.

The widget opens straight on "Reconnect Hostinger".
It reads: "Sign in to Hostinger once to keep acme.com connected. A new access key replaces the current one, and your DNS records stay as they are."
After the sign-in, our software makes a new key with the same scopes.
CustomDomain™ checks it and puts it in place of the old one in one step.
No DNS record changes, and the DNS page is never opened.
The widget says "Hostinger is reconnected".
It fires `customdomain:success` with `setupType: "reconnect"` and `reconnected: true`.
Your application gets `credential.created` with `status: "renewed"`.
A reconnect that cannot make a key leaves the current one as it was ("The new access key could not be made. The current one is unchanged.").

The old key still exists at the provider.
No provider has a way to revoke a key that we have verified, so our software does not try.
The connection's `credential` then says `old_key: "revoke_by_hand"`, with the provider's `revoke_url`.
The console's card says where to revoke it.

`POST /v1/connections/{id}/browser:start` with `"mode": "reconnect"` is the call
behind it. For a connection with no lasting key, or a provider whose key is
turned off, it answers `409 browser_reconnect_unavailable` before a browser is
started.

### Removing the key

**Remove** on the account in **Connected accounts** deletes CustomDomain™'s copy of the key and sends `credential.removed`.
`DELETE /v1/provider-accounts/{id}` does the same.
The answer carries the provider's `revoke_url`.
The key itself lives at the provider until your customer revokes it there, since no provider has an API that revokes one.
The domain stays connected and served, without repairs.
Removing the domain does the same for its key, when no other domain uses it.

## Resume after the window closes

A phone often throws a popup away while your customer reads a code in their mail app.
A window can also be closed by mistake.
While the session still waits on the customer, the widget shows **Continue signing in to Namecheap**.
That covers being about to sign in, signing in, a code prompt, or a page handed back.
It opens the same session in a new popup: the same remote browser, still on the provider's page.

The widget calls `POST /v1/connections/{id}/browser:resume` with the session it
held:

```json
{ "session_id": "bs_…", "return_origin": "https://app.example.com", "locale": "en" }
```

The answer is the one `browser:start` gives: `session_id`, `open_url` with a new single use code, `vendor` and `expires_at`.
The previous popup stops working at that moment, so one popup holds the session at a time.
Only the caller that started the session can resume it: the same end user's widget token, or the console.
Anyone else, your own API key included, gets `409 browser_not_waiting`.
That's because a popup on the session is a popup on your customer's signed-in account.
A session that is not waiting on the customer answers `409 browser_not_waiting` too.
One that ran out answers `410 browser_expired`, so start a new sign-in then.
A session can be resumed a few times, one every few seconds (`429 browser_rate_limited`).

## Resize, full screen and turning a phone

The popup can be resized, maximized, made full screen, or turned on a phone or a
tablet. Our screens stay a centered column. When the window settles at a new size,
the popup asks for the provider's page at that size with
`POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:viewport`:

```json
{ "w": 1280, "h": 800 }
```

Each side is clamped (320 to 1600 wide, 400 to 1200 high) and the answer is
`202` with the size the page is set to. It works only while your customer is in
control (signing in, or a page handed back): otherwise `409 browser_wrong_state`.
At most 6 a minute per session.

## What we never do

- **We never see or keep a password.** Your customer types into our form or the provider's page, in their own browser.
  Each key goes from that browser straight to the remote browser.
  Keystrokes, passwords and codes never pass through CustomDomain™'s servers.
- **No password or browser profile is kept.** The browser is thrown away when
  the session ends. We may keep technical records of the session to operate,
  secure and improve the service. A [lasting API key](#lasting-api-keys) made on
  the provider's own API page is kept, encrypted, for one application.
- **No run without a person.** A session never starts or goes on without your
  customer there, and it ends when the popup stops checking in.
- **Only the pages it needs.** Our software works only on three kinds of page, all described above.
  They are the provider's DNS page for that one domain, its API page where it creates a key, and its account switcher where it opens the account that holds the domain.
  Every other page of the account is refused.
- **No change without approval.** Changes made on the provider's DNS page are
  shown in full and made only after your customer approves them.
- **No terms accepted for anyone.** A provider's API terms are your customer's
  to accept.
- **Your customer sees no provider page but the four above.**
  They are a human check, an identity provider's own sign-in, the provider's terms or order, and a password asked again after the sign-in.
  Everything else is a screen of ours.
- **No check is clicked for you.** Our software never touches a human check. The
  browser hosting company may answer a standard one by itself; any other is the
  customer's to tick.

What the browser hosting company sees is the honest limit.
The browser runs at that company, and the popup names it ("Browser hosted by" and its name).
While a session runs, the company could see the screen.
That includes a Google or Apple password typed in a social sign-in.
CustomDomain™'s servers never receive any of it.

Each session records how far it got and, when it stopped short, why.
The reason comes from a fixed list.
Examples are a human check left on screen, a code that never came, the customer cancelled, or the provider's page changed.
It also records the popup's screens as steps.
No domain, typed text, page text or address is recorded.
Those counts are the **Secure sign-in** panel on the console's **Analytics** page.

## Limits

- Passkeys and security keys cannot be used.
- On a phone or a tablet the keyboard reaches the remote page over a connection
  the browser hosting company runs. A network that blocks it leaves the page
  without a keyboard, and the manual steps are the way on.
- Some records are refused before the customer signs in, with `422 browser_unsupported_records` and the types named.
  That covers record types the provider's page cannot write, and a record set you supplied yourself.
- A lasting API key is made only at the providers [listed above](#which-providers).
- At OpenSRS, only the Manage Web Interface at `manage.opensrs.net` is covered.
  A reseller's branded copy of it, a reseller's own panel and the Reseller Control Panel are not.
  No session has yet been run there signed in.
- At Spaceship, no session has yet been seen to get past the human check the
  provider's pages can show. A session that cannot get past it ends with the other
  ways to connect.
- At Squarespace, some records can only be removed together.
  These are the records Squarespace adds itself (Squarespace Defaults: the root of the domain and `www`).
  So a change there is refused, and your customer gets the manual steps.
  Records on a subdomain are changed as usual.
  Before it lets a DNS change through, Squarespace asks for the account password again, or for a code it emails to the account.
  The code opens as a dialog from Squarespace ("Verify to continue").
  Our software presses **Send code**, and the popup asks for the code on our **Enter your code** screen.
  Our software types it into Squarespace's dialog and presses **Verify**.
  It never closes the dialog.
  Ask your customer to keep the window open and to check their email for the code.
  If Squarespace's dialog cannot be read, the popup ends on its "We cannot continue the sign in here." screen.
  This has not yet been run against a live Squarespace account.
- A session lasts at most 15 minutes, or 20 once a code or a link was asked for.
  Signing in with another account starts the sign-in window again but does not
  extend that limit, and three accounts at most can be tried.

## The API behind it

The widget and the console make these calls; you do not need to. They are listed
so you can read what a session did. Every route is in the
[API reference](/docs/api-reference) under **Cloud browser**.

| Call | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| `POST /v1/connections/{id}/browser:start` | Starts a session. Takes `provider`, `return_origin`, `consent_version` (`cb-3`, the popup's own form), and optionally `locale`, `viewport_hint`, `widget_session_id` (the widget's own session, so the sign-in can be read against its device and place), `purpose` (`nameservers` for a [nameserver move](/docs/guides/nameserver-migration)) and `mode` (`reconnect` renews the connection's [lasting API key](#reconnect) and changes no record). |
| `POST /v1/connections/{id}:reconnect-link` | A single use link, for 14 days, that opens the reconnect sign-in; `{"send": true}` emails it to your customer when you gave us their address (above). Only an API key may call it: a widget token and an agent token get `403`, because the link signs in as your customer. |
| `POST /v1/connections/{id}/browser:resume` | Hands the same session to a new popup (above). |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions:redeem` | The popup trades its single use code for the session token. |
| `GET /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}` | The session as the popup shows it. While your customer signs in it carries `form` (the provider's reviewed sign-in form, with `forgotUrl`, the provider's own reset page) and `signals` (what the page asks for: a code, a link, an approval, a check, its own error, where to zoom); `handback` says why a page was handed back (`code`, `check`, `agreement` for a provider's API terms, `order` for the free order that turns the provider's API on, whose terms and Buy now are your customer's, or `account` for details the provider's account must hold before it makes a key, which your customer fills in on the provider's page and saves), `handback.relay` that the code is asked on the popup's own screen (`handback.rejected` when the provider refused the last one), `check` that a human check is on screen, `no_live_page` (`true` on every session: the popup shows the provider's page only for what the [list above](#no-live-page) keeps there) with `live_check` (a human check is on the provider's real page), and `wrongAccount` (`account`, `tried`, `max`) that the account signed in does not hold the domain. `signals` and `check` are hints for the popup's words: the server clicks or allows nothing because of them. |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:code` | Sends the code the provider asked for in a frame of its own (Squarespace's "Verify to continue"), only while the view's `handback` is `{reason: "code", relay: true}`. Body `{"code": "..."}`, 4 to 12 letters and digits. `204`; `409 browser_wrong_state` when no code is asked for or one is already on its way; `400` for a code of the wrong shape, which is never echoed. |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:switch-account` | Signs the remote browser out of the provider and opens its sign-in again, only while the view carries `wrongAccount` (otherwise `409 browser_wrong_state`). `202`; meanwhile the view's `notice` is `switching_account`. One every 10 seconds (`429 browser_rate_limited`). |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:viewport` | The popup's new size (above). |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:event` | The popup reports one of its screens, for the sign-in funnel. `204`, changes nothing about the session, at most 30 a minute. |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:signed-in`, `:approve`, `:finish-manual`, `:cancel` | "I'm signed in", the approval of the exact changes, the manual steps done, and the end of the session. `:cancel` and `:event` also take the token in the body, for a page that is closing. |

The widget's funnel names this rail `cloud_browser`, so your
[analytics](/docs/widget-sdk/reference#analytics) count it like the others.
