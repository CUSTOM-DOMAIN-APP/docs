title: Secure sign-in
description: "How secure sign-in connects a domain at a DNS provider with no sign-in or key you can use: what your customer sees, what we never see, resume, and the API."
---

Some DNS providers offer no sign-in and no API key a third party can use.
Without help, a customer of one of them types records into the provider's panel
by hand. **Secure sign-in** gives them a shorter way: they sign in to the provider in
a temporary browser that a browser hosting company runs for a few minutes, and
CustomDomain™ makes the changes on the provider's DNS page for that one domain,
then closes the browser.

<Callout title="Offered to selected workspaces for now">
  Secure sign-in runs only where it is switched on for your workspace and the
  domain's provider. `POST /v1/domains:check` says so per domain in
  `rails.cloud_browser` (present only where the deployment has it on). When
  `available` is `false`, `reason` says why, for example
  `not_enabled_for_workspace`, `provider_not_listed`, `oauth_available` (the
  provider has a sign-in of its own, which wins) or `site_not_supported` (the
  provider keeps each account on one country site, and the visitor's site is not
  served yet). The widget and the console offer it only when it is available.
</Callout>

## Who can start it

A person is always there. Only two callers may start a secure sign-in:

1. **The widget**, in your customer's browser, with a
   [widget token](/docs/authentication/widget-tokens) minted for the domain and
   the customer's `end_user_ref`. A token bound to no domain, or to another one,
   cannot start it, so its domain check answers `available: false` with
   `caller_cannot_start` and the widget shows the manual setup.
2. **The console**, from a domain's Connect page.

An AI agent, the MCP server and a plain API call are refused with
`403 browser_customer_present_only`, and their domain check never offers it.

## What your customer sees

The examples below use Namecheap as the provider and `acme.com` as the domain.
In the widget the sign-in screen has **Open secure sign in** and **Set up
manually instead**. On a computer the sign-in opens in a popup the size of every
other provider sign-in (680 by 760, centered over the page). On a phone or a
tablet it opens as a sheet over the page, and the sheet follows the visible part
of the screen, so the keyboard never covers a field.

A bar across the top shows the provider and the site the sign-in goes to. The
site gets a tick when it is the provider's own, and a warning ("This page is not
on Namecheap's site. Do not type your password here.") when it is not. The
popup's words are in the widget's 13 languages.

### Our own sign-in form

The popup opens on a sign-in form of ours: the provider's logo, "Sign in to
Namecheap", the first field named the way the provider names it (Email,
Username, Email or username, or Username or customer number), the password, and
**Sign in**. Below them are the provider's own Google, Apple or other buttons,
where it has them, and at the foot **Use Namecheap's page instead**.

Your customer types into our fields, and their own browser passes each key to
the provider's real sign-in page in the remote browser. At **Sign in** the
provider's own button is pressed. A provider that asks for the username and the
password on two pages gets them in that order.

If the provider turns the sign-in down, its own message ("That password is
incorrect", at most 120 characters, with email addresses and long numbers
masked) shows on our form with the password emptied and the username kept. If
nothing happens for 10 seconds after **Sign in**, the popup shows the provider's
page instead.

The provider's page is always one tap away. It is also what the popup shows when
we have no reviewed form for the provider, or when the customer picks **Use
Namecheap's page instead**.

### Codes, links and approvals

The remote browser is a new device to every provider, so most providers ask for
a code. Each prompt gets one screen of ours:

| The provider asks for | The popup shows |
| --- | --- |
| A code by email, text or authenticator app | Where the code went ("Check your email for the code from Namecheap."), a code field and **Continue**. |
| A sign-in link by email | "Open the email from Namecheap and tap the link, then come back here." When the customer comes back, the provider's page is nudged once. If it still waits 8 seconds later: "Signed in on this device instead? Paste the link from the email." |
| An approval on another device | "Approve the sign in on your phone", with nothing to press. |
| A passkey or a security key | "Passkeys cannot be used here" and **Use another way**. A passkey cannot reach a browser on another machine. |

Codes take time to arrive, so a code or a link keeps the session open: the
sign-in window runs at least 10 minutes from the moment the prompt appears, and
the whole session can run 20 minutes instead of 15.

### Google, Apple and other sign-ins

Where the provider's sign-in page offers "Sign in with Google" (or Apple,
Facebook, Amazon and the like) and we have reviewed that provider's sign-ins
(GoDaddy's Google, Apple, Facebook and Amazon buttons, for example), our form
shows the same buttons. Pressing one presses the provider's own, and the
identity provider's page shows in the popup, including a window it opens. Your
customer signs in there; **Back to GoDaddy** returns to the provider's page when
the sign-in took the whole tab. Only your customer goes there: the
software that makes the changes never visits an identity provider's page.

If no Google window appears (some pages use the browser's own account chooser,
which a fresh browser has no account for), the customer signs in with the
provider's password instead.

### Human checks

Some providers show a "Verify you are human" check to a browser they have not
seen before. The popup shows the provider's page with **Tick the box**, and your
customer ticks it. We never solve, click, work around or hide a check. If it has
not cleared after 45 seconds, **Try again** reloads the view and **Set it up
another way** leads to the manual steps.

### After the sign-in

Once the sign-in is through, the popup says **Signed in** and then shows one line
for each step ("Reading your DNS records", "Adding records", "Making the
changes", "Checking the result") over the records, which tick as they are made.
Changes made on the provider's DNS page are first shown in full on a **Review**
screen ("Review the changes for acme.com"), and nothing changes until your
customer presses **Approve and make these changes**. Then **Done.**

Nobody watches the provider's page after the sign-in. If the provider asks for
the password again, asks for a code, or shows a check in the middle of the work,
the page is handed back ("Namecheap asks for your password again."). Your
customer answers it the same way, and the work goes on once the prompt is gone.

### Signed in to the wrong account

The changes can be made only in the account that holds the domain's DNS. If the
provider's page shows that the domain uses DNS somewhere else, the popup says
"acme.com does not use Namecheap's DNS, so its records are edited elsewhere."
and the session ends with `browser_not_provider_dns`. If the domain's DNS page
cannot be reached from the account, the popup moves to the manual steps with the
browser still open. Either way your customer can start again and sign in to the
account that holds the domain, or add the records by hand.

## Resume after the window closes

A phone often throws a popup away while your customer reads a code in their mail
app, and a window can be closed by mistake. While the session still waits on the
customer (about to sign in, signing in, a code prompt, or a page handed back),
the widget shows **Continue signing in to Namecheap**. It opens the same
session in a new popup: the same remote browser, still on the provider's page.

The widget calls `POST /v1/connections/{id}/browser:resume` with the session it
held:

```json
{ "session_id": "bs_…", "return_origin": "https://app.example.com", "locale": "en" }
```

The answer is the one `browser:start` gives (`session_id`, `open_url` with a new
single use code, `vendor`, `expires_at`), and the previous popup stops working at
that moment, so one popup holds the session at a time. A session that is not
waiting on the customer answers `409 browser_not_waiting`, and one that ran out
`410 browser_expired`; start a new sign-in then. A session can be resumed a few
times, one every few seconds (`429 browser_rate_limited`).

## Resize, full screen and turning a phone

The popup can be resized, maximized, made full screen, or turned on a phone or a
tablet. Our screens stay a centered column. When the window settles at a new size,
the popup asks for the provider's page at that size with
`POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:viewport`:

```json
{ "w": 1280, "h": 800 }
```

Each side is clamped (320 to 1600 wide, 400 to 1200 high) and the answer is
`202` with the size the page is set to. It works only while your customer is in
control (signing in, or a page handed back): otherwise `409 browser_wrong_state`.
At most 6 a minute per session.

## What we never do

- **We never see or keep a password.** Your customer types into our form or the
  provider's page in their own browser, and each key goes from that browser
  straight to the remote browser. Keystrokes, passwords and codes never pass
  through CustomDomain™'s servers.
- **Nothing is stored.** No password, cookie, recording or browser profile is
  kept, and the browser is thrown away when the session ends.
- **No run without a person.** A session never starts or goes on without your
  customer there, and it ends when the popup stops checking in.
- **Only the one DNS page.** Our software works only on the provider's DNS page
  for that one domain. Every other page of the account is refused.
- **No change without approval.** Changes made on the provider's DNS page are
  shown in full and made only after your customer approves them.
- **No check is solved for you.** A human check is always the customer's to tick.

What the browser hosting company sees is the honest limit. The browser runs at
that company, which the popup names ("Browser hosted by" and its name), and while
a session runs the company could see the screen, which includes a Google or Apple
password typed in a social sign-in. CustomDomain™'s servers never receive any of
it.

Each session records how far it got and, when it stopped short, why, from a
fixed list (a human check left on screen, a code that never came, the customer
cancelled, the provider's page changed, and so on), together with the popup's
screens as steps. No domain, typed text, page text or address is recorded. Those
counts are the **Secure sign-in** panel on the console's **Analytics** page.

## Limits

- Passkeys and security keys cannot be used.
- On a phone or a tablet the keyboard reaches the remote page over a connection
  the browser hosting company runs. A network that blocks it leaves the page
  without a keyboard, and the manual steps are the way on.
- Record types the provider's page cannot write, and a record set you supplied
  yourself, are refused before the customer signs in
  (`422 browser_unsupported_records`, with the types named).
- A session lasts at most 15 minutes, or 20 once a code or a link was asked for.

## The API behind it

The widget and the console make these calls; you do not need to. They are listed
so you can read what a session did. Every route is in the
[API reference](/docs/api-reference) under **Cloud browser**.

| Call | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| `POST /v1/connections/{id}/browser:start` | Starts a session. Takes `provider`, `return_origin`, `consent_version` (`cb-3`, the popup's own form), and optionally `locale`, `viewport_hint`, `widget_session_id` (the widget's own session, so the sign-in can be read against its device and place) and `purpose` (`nameservers` for a [nameserver move](/docs/guides/nameserver-migration)). |
| `POST /v1/connections/{id}/browser:resume` | Hands the same session to a new popup (above). |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions:redeem` | The popup trades its single use code for the session token. |
| `GET /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}` | The session as the popup shows it. While your customer signs in it carries `form` (the provider's reviewed sign-in form) and `signals` (what the page asks for: a code, a link, an approval, a check, its own error, where to zoom); `handback` says why a page was handed back, and `check` that a human check is on screen. `signals` and `check` are hints for the popup's words: the server clicks or allows nothing because of them. |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:viewport` | The popup's new size (above). |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:event` | The popup reports one of its screens, for the sign-in funnel. `204`, changes nothing about the session, at most 30 a minute. |
| `POST /v1/browser-sessions/{sid}:signed-in`, `:approve`, `:finish-manual`, `:cancel` | "I'm signed in", the approval of the exact changes, the manual steps done, and the end of the session. `:cancel` and `:event` also take the token in the body, for a page that is closing. |

The widget's funnel names this rail `cloud_browser`, so your
[analytics](/docs/widget-sdk/reference#analytics) count it like the others.
